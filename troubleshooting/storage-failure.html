Bags lost their crunch: diagnosing storage failures

Reviewed by Jack Ferrence, Sublime Pantry Editorial
Published August 31, 2026

Food safety note: This article is educational, not professional food-safety, legal, or medical advice. Freeze-drying does not kill pathogens, and cottage food rules vary by state. Confirm requirements with your state or local food authority before selling anything.

A batch that was perfect on packaging day and soft three months later failed at one of exactly five points. Work down the list, and if you sell, treat every failed bag as a process defect to trace, because your customers’ bags came off the same table.

1. The food was never dry enough

The most common root cause hides upstream of packaging. Food that passed a casual glance but not a real cut test carries moisture into the bag, where it equalizes and softens everything. Check: open a suspect bag and cut-test several pieces. Uniform slight softness across the bag points to residual moisture, not a leak. Fix: re-dry, re-test, re-bag; then tighten your end-of-cycle dryness testing.

2. The packaging wasn’t a real barrier

Zip-top bags, thin food-saver plastic, and decorative pouches are not long-term freeze-dry storage — they pass moisture and oxygen slowly but relentlessly. The standard is mylar in the 5–7.5 mil range with a metallized barrier layer, mason jars vacuum-sealed, or PETE with absorbers. Thin printed pouches sold for candy resale are fine for weeks of retail turnover, not years of pantry. Fix: match packaging to intended life: barrier mylar for storage, and inform retail customers of a realistic best-by window.

3. The oxygen absorber was spent or undersized

Absorbers die in open air: a bowl of absorbers left out during a long bagging session is a bowl of warm iron filings. Sizing matters too: roughly 100 cc per quart of container volume (300–500 cc for a one-gallon bag), erring high because freeze-dried food is airy. Check: healthy sealed bags tighten slightly within 24 hours as the absorber works; a bag that stayed pillowy never had a working absorber (or has a leak — see #4). Fix: buy absorbers in small sealed packs, open one pack per session, and reseal spares immediately.

4. The seal itself leaked

A wrinkle, a crumb, or a stray thread of candy in the seal line is a slow leak with a years-long fuse. Check: press gently on the bag and watch the seal line; inspect for channels. Fix: seal wide and wrinkle-free, wipe the seal area before closing, double-seal high-value bags, and spot-check each session’s output the next day.

5. The environment beat the packaging

Even good packaging loses eventually to heat and light. Guidance converges on storage below 70 °F, dark, and dry: a closet, not a garage that hits 95 °F all summer. High-fat foods deserve special mention: fat oxidizes regardless of moisture control, so bacon-adjacent experiments and oily leftovers have inherently short horizons; extension guidance flags high-fat foods as poor freeze-drying candidates in the first place.

The one-day-later habit that catches almost everything

Twenty-four hours after packaging, walk the day’s bags: each should be slightly drawn in, seals clean, contents audibly crisp when tilted. That single check catches spent absorbers, seal channels, and wet batches while they’re still one batch — log it as a pass/fail in your Batch Log and storage failures become rare events instead of sad discoveries.
