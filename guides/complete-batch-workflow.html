The complete batch workflow: from tray prep to sealed mylar

By Sublime Pantry Editorial
Published August 31, 2026

Food safety note: This article is educational, not professional food-safety, legal, or medical advice. Freeze-drying does not kill pathogens, and cottage food rules vary by state. Confirm requirements with your state or local food authority before selling anything.

A freeze-drying batch is won or lost at its edges: the prep before the cycle and the half hour after the door opens. The machine handles the middle. This guide walks the whole workflow in order, with the checkpoints that separate a 25-year pantry shelf from a bag of stale disappointment.

Understand what the machine is actually doing

Home freeze dryers work in three stages, per University of Minnesota Extension. First, food is frozen solid — typically to between −30 and −50 °F. Second, in primary drying, a vacuum pump lowers the chamber pressure until the ice sublimates: crystals turn directly to vapor, removing up to about 90% of the water. Third, secondary drying gently warms the trays to pull out another 1–2% of bound moisture. That last small fraction is the difference between shelf-stable and spoiled, which is why “it looks dry” is never the standard.

One safety fact frames everything else: freeze-drying does not kill pathogens. Extension guidance is blunt about this — food that was unsafe going in is unsafe coming out, and organisms paused by drying can resume growing after rehydration. Clean prep, fresh ingredients, and normal kitchen temperature control matter exactly as much as they do in ordinary cooking.

Prep: uniformity beats everything

Cut pieces to a uniform, modest thickness — thinner food dries faster and more evenly, and mixed sizes on one tray guarantee that something finishes wet. Load trays level and to the machine’s rated weight, not above it. Group similar foods in a batch when you can: a tray of dense cooked meat chunks and a tray of raspberries want very different amounts of dry time, and the batch only ends once.

The cycle: mostly patience

Expect most batches to run somewhere between 13 and 35 hours depending on food, load, and machine (high-sugar candy is the outlier, finishing in a few hours). Resist opening early. The expensive mistakes happen at the end, not the middle.

The dryness test: the checkpoint that matters

When the cycle ends, test before you trust. Extension guidance: take the thickest pieces from the center trays, cut them in half, and inspect the middle for dark, cold, or moist spots. A properly finished piece snaps or crumbles; anything cool, flexible, or dark in the center means moisture remains. If you find any, return all the trays and add more dry time — a marginal piece you can find implies marginal pieces you can’t. Properly freeze-dried food is extraordinarily dry: water activity readings for finished product typically land between 0.08 and 0.33, far below anything that supports microbial growth.

Package inside the window

Freeze-dried food is aggressively hygroscopic — it starts pulling moisture back out of the air the moment the door opens. Package promptly; don’t leave trays sitting out for hours, and on humid days move fast or stage finished trays in the (off, sealed) chamber.

The standard for long-term storage is a true moisture and oxygen barrier:

  • Mylar bags, 5–7.5 mil, with a metallized layer — the workhorse for storage and the default for cottage sellers.
  • Vacuum-sealed mason jars — excellent for foods you’ll open and reseal, and for the pantry-visible stuff.
  • PETE containers with oxygen absorbers — acceptable per extension guidance, less common in practice.

Size oxygen absorbers to the container, not the food: roughly 100 cc per quart of volume as a rule of thumb, which works out to about 300–500 cc for a one-gallon mylar bag. Freeze-dried food is airy, so lean toward the high side rather than the low. Seal with an impulse sealer (an iron or hair straightener works in a pinch), squeeze out what air you can first, and make the seal continuous and wrinkle-free edge to edge. A good sign within the first day: the bag tightens slightly as the absorber works. A pillow-soft bag at hour 24 means a seal failure — open, replace the absorber, reseal.

Label everything with the food, prep method (raw, cooked, powdered), and date, then store below 70 °F, dark and dry. Commercially freeze-dried foods claim shelf lives up to 25 years; home results depend on your dryness, packaging, and storage, so treat those claims as a ceiling, not a promise — and note that high-fat foods oxidize and fall well short of it.

The workflow, on one card

Prep uniform → load level → run full cycle → cut-test center-tray pieces → extra dry time if in doubt → package within the hour in real barrier packaging with sized absorbers → verify seals at 24 hours → label and store cool, dark, dry.
