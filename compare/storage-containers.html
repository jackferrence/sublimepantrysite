Mylar bags vs. mason jars vs. PETE: freeze-dried storage compared

Reviewed by Jack Ferrence, Sublime Pantry Editorial
Published August 31, 2026

How this comparison was made: criteria are stated up front and every figure is drawn from the manufacturer documentation or expert sources listed at the bottom of the page. We have not yet bench-tested these products ourselves — this is a specification and documentation comparison, and it says so wherever judgment is involved. Criteria: moisture and oxygen barrier, cost per stored unit, resealability and daily-use ergonomics, durability and light protection, fitness for cottage retail.

Extension guidance names three legitimate homes for freeze-dried food: mylar bags, vacuum-sealed mason jars, and PETE containers, all with oxygen absorbers, all hermetically sealed. They are not interchangeable. Here’s how they compare on the criteria that decide the choice, and which to use for what.

Long-term storage: mylar, with discipline

For sealed-and-forgotten storage, thick metallized mylar wins: an excellent moisture, oxygen, and light barrier at a low per-bag cost. The qualifier is discipline, since its performance depends entirely on execution: bags in the 5–7.5 mil range (not thin decorative pouches), absorbers sized around 100 cc per quart (300–500 cc per gallon bag), a clean wrinkle-free seal, and a next-day check that the bag has drawn in slightly. Stored inside bins or buckets (mylar’s one weakness is puncture) below 70 °F and dark, this is the system behind every serious long-term pantry.

Open-and-close pantry use: jars

Glass is a perfect barrier, and jars are the only option here that’s pleasant to open weekly: vacuum-seal, use some, re-vacuum. The costs are light sensitivity (store dark or accept faded, degraded contents), breakage, and shelf-space economics. Jars shine for the crunchy-snack rotation and powdered ingredients; they make little sense for 40 pounds of long-horizon food storage.

PETE: the situational third

Food-grade PETE bottles and jars with absorbers are extension-approved and durable, but their barrier performance sits below mylar and glass, and they’re usually clear. They’re a fine home for bulk staples you’ll cycle through in a year or two, and a poor one for decade-scale storage of anything delicate.

For sellers, the answer is narrower

Cottage retail runs on printed mylar-style pouches: light, cheap to ship to you, attractive on a market table. Two honest caveats. First, many retail candy pouches are thinner than storage-grade mylar; that’s acceptable for product that sells within weeks, but label a realistic best-by window rather than borrowing “25-year” storage claims from thick-mylar practice. Second, your absorber-and-seal discipline is now a customer-experience issue: a stale bag isn’t lost inventory, it’s a lost repeat buyer. Our storage-failure checklist doubles as a pre-market QA list.

Bottom line

Mylar for the deep pantry, jars for the working shelf, PETE for cycled bulk, and retail pouches (honestly dated) for the market table. All four fail the same way: a spent absorber or a wrinkled seal. The container matters less than the closing ritual.
